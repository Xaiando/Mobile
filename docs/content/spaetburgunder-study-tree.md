## 0. Fast track for a blind tasting

A plan for the weeks before a blind event. Each session is 45–90 minutes. If time is short, do sessions 1, 3 and 6.

| # | Session | What to do | Tree sections |

## 1. Identity

### 1.1 Names

- **Spätburgunder** is the German name of Pinot Noir. German wine law lists the variety as *Blauer Spätburgunder*. A wine without a protected origin (PDO or PGI) may not name it, nor any of its synonyms [WeinV §42(2)].
- **Synonyms.** Geisenheim lists Pinot Noir, Pinot Nero, Blauburgunder, Klevner, Klebrot, Pinot Tinto and Pignol [HGU]. The OIV's list adds Blauer Burgunder and Spätburgunder, among many others [OIV p. 15]. German labels also use *Pinot Noir*. *To verify:* the synonyms admitted on German labels, in the national variety register.
- **The name pairs it with Frühburgunder**, the earlier-ripening Pinot [DWI-SB].

### 1.2 Origin and history

- **Origins.** The OIV traces Pinot Noir to 14th-century Burgundy. Crosses between Pinot and Gouais Blanc produced 21 varieties grown in France [OIV p. 14]. Geisenheim calls it probably the parent form of the other Burgunder varieties, and a parent of Chardonnay and Auxerrois [HGU].
- **The route into Germany.** It came up the Rhône valley and through the Burgundian Gate into the Upper Rhine valley. German tradition holds that Emperor Charles III brought it to Lake Constance in 884, under the name *Clävner* [HGU; DWI-SB also gives 884]. Medieval monasteries then spread it [HGU]. The 884 date is older than the OIV's documented origin, and is best learnt as a tradition.
- **The Rheingau.** The sources differ on when it arrived:
  - Geisenheim: Cistercian monks of Kloster Eberbach brought it around 1440, as *Klebrot* [HGU];
  - the DWI: the 13th century [DWI-SB];
  - the regional association: Assmannshausen has grown red wine since the 13th century [RWV].
- **The Ahr.** The DWI says it reached the Ahr in the 18th century [DWI-SB]. Geisenheim adds the *Kastenholz* type, an old Ahr form said to have come from Burgundy through the monks of Kornelimünster near Aachen (§5.3) [HGU].

### 1.3 The Burgunder family in Germany

German planted areas in 2024 [DWI-Stat, table 4]:

| Variety on the label | Also known as | Colour | Area 2024 | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spätburgunder | Pinot Noir | red | 11,437 ha | Germany's most planted red variety |
| Frühburgunder (*Blauer Frühburgunder*) | Pinot Noir Précoce | red | 212 ha | The early-ripening form; 30 ha of it are in the Ahr |
| Schwarzriesling (*Müllerrebe*) | Pinot Meunier | red | 1,598 ha | 1,119 ha of it in Württemberg, 10 % of that region |
| Grauburgunder | Pinot Gris | white (grey-pink skin) | 8,404 ha | |

Geisenheim describes Pinot Noir as probably the original form of the other Burgunder varieties [HGU]. *To verify:* that these colour and form variants share one Pinot genotype, in the VIVC variety catalogue.

### 1.4 Vine biology

- **Budburst.** The OIV calls it early-budding, so spring frost is a risk, though it resists winter frost [OIV p. 14]. Geisenheim's German ratings [HGU]:
  - budburst: medium;
  - flowering and ripening: medium to late;
  - vigour: medium to strong.

  Either way, the frost of 21–26 April 2024 struck after an unusually early budburst [DWI-2024].
- **Warmth.** It ripens quickly in warm climates [OIV p. 14]. In warmer, more southern regions the wines lose quality: alcohol rises and the aromas become less distinctive [OIV p. 15]. That is why cool sites and cool years give it balance.
- **Cool, humid weather at flowering** causes *millerandage* (uneven berry size) and *coulure* (poor fruit set). Clay and humid soils do not suit it [OIV p. 14].
- **Site demands are high.** It wants well-aired sites with soils that warm easily. Because it reacts to drought, it also needs deep soils with enough water [HGU].
- **Disease and pests.** It is susceptible to downy mildew, botrytis and leafhoppers [OIV p. 14].
  - In wet autumns the dense bunches of the traditional clones rot quickly [HGU].
  - Rotten fruit loses much of its colour, so the wine turns pale and brownish instead of ruby [HGU].
  - Clone choice is the German answer (§5.3).
- **Low yields.** Fertility is low, with small clusters and very small berries: 3–9 t/ha in Burgundy [OIV p. 14].
- **The grapes** are high in sugar and moderately acidic, and the skins are rich in polyphenols. The wines are lightly coloured and age well in barrel [OIV p. 14].
- **In temperate zones** the wines are light to moderately coloured and moderately acidic, known for aromatic complexity and finesse [OIV p. 15].
- **A demanding variety.** The DWI calls it demanding in soil, climate and weather [DWI-SB].
- **Why the wine is pale.** Pinot Noir lacks acylated anthocyanins, a varietal marker used in authenticity analysis. *To verify:* against the anthocyanin-profiling literature (P3).

## 3. Law and labels

German wine law is the Weingesetz [WeinG] and the Weinverordnung [WeinV], within EU law. The WeinV was last amended on **24 August 2026**. That amendment changed the rules for Erstes and Großes Gewächs (§3.5).

### 3.1 The 13 specified regions (*bestimmte Anbaugebiete*)

The law lists them in this order: Ahr, Baden, Franken, Hessische Bergstraße, Mittelrhein, Mosel, Nahe, Pfalz, Rheingau, Rheinhessen, Saale-Unstrut, Sachsen, Württemberg [WeinG §3(1)].

### 3.2 Categories and Prädikate

- **Categories.** *Qualitätswein* is a PDO wine that passes an analytical and sensory test. *Prädikatswein* is a PDO wine awarded a Prädikat in an official test. *Landwein* is a PGI wine [WeinG §2 no. 24, 25, 27].
- **The Prädikate** [WeinG §20]:
  - *Kabinett*: not enriched; the higher Prädikate add conditions on the grapes;
  - *Spätlese*: fully ripe grapes from a late harvest;
  - *Auslese*: fully ripe or nobly rotten grapes;
  - *Beerenauslese*: nobly rotten, or at least overripe, berries;
  - *Trockenbeerenauslese*: largely shrivelled, nobly rotten berries; overripeness suffices in exceptional years;
  - *Eiswein*: grapes frozen at harvest and pressing;
  - Beerenauslese and Trockenbeerenauslese must be picked by hand.
- **Minimum natural alcohol** is set by each state, by region and variety, above national floors [WeinG §17(3)]:
  - zone A: Qualitätswein 7.0 %, Prädikatswein 9.5 %;
  - zone B: 8.0 % and 10.0 %;
  - the Ahr, Mittelrhein, Mosel and Saale-Unstrut may go lower for certain varieties and sites.
- **Minimum actual alcohol** [WeinG §17(1)]: 7 % for Qualitätswein, Kabinett, Spätlese and Auslese; 5.5 % for Beerenauslese, Trockenbeerenauslese and Eiswein.
- **Baden is the only German region in EU wine-growing zone B.** All others are in zone A [EU-1308, Annex VII Appendix 1; DWI-Baden].
- **Top dry reds are usually Qualitätswein**, not Prädikatswein. The VDP keeps Prädikat names for wines with natural sweetness. *To verify* in [VDP].

### 3.3 The origin pyramid

Smaller units are stricter [WeinV §39(1); WeinG §23]:

| Level | On the label | Rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Gebietswein** | The region (Anbaugebiet) | The PDO's own rules |
| **Regionalwein** | A *Bereich* or *Großlage*, preceded by the word "Region" in the same colour, font and size | §39(1) no. 1 |
| **Ortswein** | A village or part of a village | At least the Kabinett natural minimum alcohol; not sold to consumers before **15 December** of the harvest year (§39(1) no. 2) |
| **Lagenwein** | An *Einzellage*, or a smaller unit registered in the vineyard register, always with the village name | At least the Kabinett minimum; only the varieties that the product specification lists; not sold before **1 March** of the following year (§39(1) no. 3) |

- Product specifications may be stricter, for example on yields [WeinV §39(1)].
- **A Lage** is a single vineyard (*Einzellage*) or a group of them (*Großlage*). **A Bereich** groups Lagen that give similar wines [WeinG §2 no. 22–23].
- A Lage must normally cover at least **5 ha** to be registered [WeinV §29(1)].
- A PGI wine (Landwein) may name no unit smaller than its PGI [WeinG §23(1a)].
- **Variety and vintage.** A variety or vintage may be named when at least 85 % of the wine comes from it [EU-2019/33 Art. 49–50]. For sweetened wine the sweetening products count too [WeinV §§42–43].

### 3.4 VDP: a private classification (L-19)

The VDP is a private association. Its terms are its own trademarks, not law. The app explains them descriptively and never uses them as its own labels [VDP]:

| VDP tier | Maximum yield | Dry wine from this tier |
|---|---|---|
| VDP.GUTSWEIN | 75 hl/ha | |
| VDP.ORTSWEIN | 75 hl/ha | |
| VDP.ERSTE LAGE® | 60 hl/ha | carries the "1G" logo |
| VDP.GROSSE LAGE® | 50 hl/ha | VDP.GROSSES GEWÄCHS®, carrying the "GG" logo |

- **Harvest.** Grapes are picked selectively by hand.
- **GG rules.** A GG has at most 9 g/l residual sugar. A red GG spends at least 12 months in oak and is released from **1 September of the second calendar year after the harvest**; a white GG from 1 September of the year after.
- **Varieties.** Each region lists the varieties allowed for its Große Lage sites. The Rheingau's list is Riesling and Spätburgunder.
- **History.** This four-tier system dates from January 2012.

### 3.5 Erstes Gewächs and Großes Gewächs under the 2026 ordinance

- **Now quality marks.** Since the amendment of 24 August 2026, *Erstes Gewächs* and *Großes Gewächs* are quality marks (*Gütezeichen*) of the **Komitee – Klassifikation Erster und Großer Lagen in Deutschland e. V.** [WeinV §30(2) no. 2].
- **Who may use them.** Only PDO wines that meet the committee's award rules [WeinV §30(4)]. Those rules must:
  - be objective and non-discriminatory;
  - evaluate and classify sites;
  - reflect regional specifics such as site boundaries, site-specific varieties and methods;
  - where a region has a recognised producer organisation, be set in agreement with it for that region.
- **Approval.** The committee's procedural and testing rules need the approval of the Federal Ministry of Agriculture, after the states are heard. They are then published on the committee's website [WeinV §30(5)].
- **Look-alikes are banned.** Terms that can be confused with a mark are prohibited [WeinV §30(7)].
- **Transition.** Until the 2030 vintage, or until the committee's rules take effect if that is sooner, the terms may still be used under the rules in force up to 1 September 2026 [WeinV §54(23)]. Those were the terms of the 2021 reform, defined per region by the protection associations.
- **Two systems.** *Großes Gewächs* (a legal quality mark) and VDP.GROSSES GEWÄCHS® (a private trademark) must be told apart. *To verify:* the committee's published rules, once they take effect, for yields, varieties and release dates (P2).

### 3.6 Wine types and style terms

[WeinV §32]:

| Term | Rule | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Rotwein** | Only red grapes | All regions |
| **Roséwein**, Rosé | Only red grapes; pale to light red | All regions; a German rosé must say Rosé, Roséwein or Rotling |
| **Weißherbst** | Qualitätswein or Prädikatswein from **one red variety**, at least **95 % light-pressed** must; the variety is named in the same type, size and colour; never also called Roséwein | All regions |
| **Blanc de Noir(s)** | Red grapes pressed like white wine, with the typical colour of white wine; for PDO or PGI wine, PDO liqueur wine and sparkling wines | All regions |
| **Rotling** | Pale to light red; white and red grapes blended, also as mash | All regions |
| **Schillerwein** | A Rotling Qualitätswein or Prädikatswein | Württemberg only |
| **Badisch Rotgold** | A Rotling with the addition "Grauburgunder und Spätburgunder" | Baden only |
| **Schieler** | A Rotling | Sachsen only |

### 3.7 Oak, slope and sweetness terms

- **Oak.** "Fermented", "matured" or "aged" in a wooden container, including the Barrique terms, are allowed only when [WeinV §32(8)]:
  - at least 75 % of the wine spent that time in that kind of container;
  - red wine spent at least **6 months** there (4 months for other wine);
  - for "Barrique", the barrel holds no more than **350 litres**.
- **Steillage** (steep-slope wine) requires vineyards of at least **30 %** gradient. **Terrassenlage** requires terraces on such slopes. The two may not be combined [WeinV §34b].
- **Sweetness of still wine** [EU-2019/33 Annex III Part B]:
  - *trocken*: up to 4 g/l, or up to 9 g/l if the total acidity is at most 2 g/l below the sugar;
  - *halbtrocken*: up to 12 g/l, or up to 18 g/l if the acidity is at most 10 g/l below the sugar;
  - *lieblich*: up to 45 g/l;
  - *süß*: at least 45 g/l.
  - *Feinherb* is not one of these terms.

### 3.8 Sparkling wine

- **Winzersekt** is a *Sekt b.A.* made by traditional bottle fermentation. It rests at least **9 months on its lees** in the same estate and is disgorged. It comes from the estate's own grapes (or a producer group's), and states the estate, variety and vintage [WeinV §34a(3)].
- **Crémant** follows EU rules and must name its PDO [WeinV §34a(1)].
- **Pinot for sparkling.** Spätburgunder is a common base, as Blanc de Noirs or rosé. The Pfalz rated its 2025 Burgunder grapes for sparkling wine excellent [DWI-2025].

## 4. Regions and sites

Each region lists its facts first, then *to verify* items and heuristics. Site facts become `site` nodes with location items. G12 draws them on maps: the RLP vineyard register for the Ahr, Mosel, Nahe, Pfalz, Rheinhessen and Mittelrhein; other states' registers, or the point fallback (GEO-9), for the rest.

### 4.1 Baden: Germany's Spätburgunder heartland

- **Size.** 15,454 ha (2024). Spätburgunder has 4,910 ha, 31.8 %, the region's leading variety [DWI-Stat].
- **Shape.** The region runs about 400 km, from the Tauber to Lake Constance. It is Germany's third-largest region, and the only one in **EU zone B**, where must-weight minimums are higher [DWI-Baden; EU-1308].
- **Nine Bereiche:** Tauberfranken, Badische Bergstraße, Kraichgau, Ortenau, Breisgau, Kaiserstuhl, Tuniberg, Markgräflerland and Bodensee. Baden has 16 Großlagen and 306 Einzellagen [DWI-Baden].
- **The Kaiserstuhl** has some of Germany's warmest places [DWI-Baden].
  - It is the ruin of a Miocene volcano, active about 19–15 million years ago, built of tephrite lavas and tuffs [LGRB].
  - Loess up to 30 m thick, locally 60 m, covers much of it [LGRB].
  - Wines from the volcanic soils and from the loess terraces are compared on the Kaiserstuhl. **(H)**
- **Other Bereiche** (*to verify* against the Baden-Württemberg register and the Badischer Weinbauverband):
  - the Ortenau's granite slopes, where Spätburgunder and Riesling lead;
  - the Tuniberg's limestone ridge near Freiburg;
  - Lake Constance (Bodensee), with Spätburgunder and Müller-Thurgau; the tradition of 884 places Spätburgunder's German start here (§1.2).
- **Style.** **(H)** Among German Spätburgunder, Baden's are often the ripest and roundest: dark cherry, fuller body and more alcohol, especially from the Kaiserstuhl in warm years.

### 4.2 Pfalz

- **Size.** 23,787 ha. Spätburgunder has 1,757 ha, 7.4 %, Germany's second-largest Spätburgunder area [DWI-Stat].
- **Red wine.** The Pfalz has Germany's largest red-wine area: 7,535 ha in 2023 [DWI-Profile].
- **To verify:**
  - its two Bereiche, Mittelhaardt–Deutsche Weinstraße and Südliche Weinstraße (RLP register);
  - the Haardt, the Palatinate Forest's eastern edge, sheltering the vineyards from the west.
- **Schweigener Sonnenberg** (*to verify* in the RLP register): an Einzellage that crosses the French border. It includes vineyards around Wissembourg in Alsace, among them the historic Kammerberg, which were merged into it in 1971.
- **Style.** **(H)** The southern Pfalz's limestone sites give ripe but taut, fine-grained Spätburgunder. The region is warm and dry.

### 4.3 Rheinhessen

- **Size.** Germany's largest region, 27,671 ha. Spätburgunder has 1,529 ha, 5.5 % [DWI-Stat].
- **To verify:**
  - its three Bereiche: Bingen, Nierstein and Wonnegau;
  - Ingelheim's red-wine tradition, on limestone.

### 4.4 Württemberg

- **Size.** 11,179 ha, 64 % red [DWI-Stat].
- **Varieties.** Riesling (2,084 ha), Trollinger (1,761 ha) and Lemberger (1,727 ha) lead Spätburgunder (1,284 ha, 11.5 %). Schwarzriesling has 1,119 ha [DWI-Stat].
- **Rotling.** Württemberg's Rotling alone may be called **Schillerwein** [WeinV §32(7)].
- **To verify:**
  - its six Bereiche: Remstal-Stuttgart, Württembergisch Unterland, Kocher-Jagst-Tauber, Oberer Neckar, Württembergischer Bodensee and Bayerischer Bodensee;
  - its Keuper soils.
- **Why it matters.** **(H)** This is the region whose other reds are most often mistaken for Spätburgunder in a German flight (§10.2).

### 4.5 Ahr: the red-wine valley

- **Size.** 533 ha (2024), 79 % red, the highest red share of any German region [DWI-Stat].
- **Varieties.** Spätburgunder 346 ha (64.9 %), Riesling 45 ha, Frühburgunder 30 ha [DWI-Stat]. The DWI counts 40 Einzellagen [DWI-Ahr].
- **Where.** Vineyards follow only about 24 km of the river Ahr, in the north of Rhineland-Palatinate. The main town is Bad Neuenahr-Ahrweiler [WoG-Ahr].
- **Soils** [WoG-Ahr]:
  - deep loess in the lower (eastern) valley;
  - slate, volcanic stone and rocky soils in the middle (western) valley.
  - *To verify:* the greywacke (hard, grey sandstone) of the middle valley.
- **Climate.** The steep, terraced sites are called greenhouse-like [WoG-Ahr].
- **Floods and harvests:**
  - **The flood of July 2021.** Extreme rain on 14–15 July 2021 (the low "Bernd") caused catastrophic flooding along the Ahr [DWD-2021].
  - **2024:** frost cut the harvest by 64 %. Spätburgunder yielded 35 hl/ha at 86 °Oechsle on average [DWI-2024].
  - **2025:** cool nights and harmonious acidity; Frühburgunder reached about 100 °Oechsle [DWI-2025].
- **Style.** **(H)** Ahr Spätburgunder from slate is often dark-fruited, spicy and smoky-mineral, concentrated yet fresh. Top wines show clear oak.

### 4.6 Rheingau

- **Size.** 3,180 ha, 85 : 15 white to red. Spätburgunder has 400 ha, 12.6 % [DWI-Stat].
- **Assmannshäuser Höllenberg** [RWV]:
  - 46 ha of red vines on slopes of **30–60 %**, facing south-southwest;
  - stony, gritty clay slate (*Tonschiefer*) mixed with loess loam and weathered Taunus quartzite, which stores heat;
  - red wine here since the 13th century; the regional association calls it one of Germany's best red-wine sites;
  - it lies in the Upper Middle Rhine Valley World Heritage area;
  - it qualifies as *Steillage* (§3.7).
- **Monks.** Geisenheim credits the Cistercians of Kloster Eberbach with bringing it into the Rheingau around 1440 (§1.2) [HGU].
- **Vintages.** In 2024 the Burgunder varieties had very low yields almost throughout the region [DWI-2024]. In 2025, harvest began around 20 August, the earliest since records began in 1955 [DWI-2025].
- **Style.** **(H)** Höllenberg wines are fine and spicy, firm and long-lived.

### 4.7 Franken

- **Size.** 6,128 ha, 17 % red. Spätburgunder has 277 ha, 4.5 %; Domina is the leading red (290 ha) [DWI-Stat].
- **Churfranken** (*to verify* in the Bavarian register), on the lower Main near Miltenberg:
  - red sandstone (*Buntsandstein*) terraces;

## 5. Viticulture

### 5.1 Site and climate

- **Why sites matter.** The variety is demanding in soil, climate and weather [DWI-SB], so German growers give it warm sites:
  - steep, south-facing slopes: *Steillage* means at least 30 % gradient [WeinV §34b];
  - heat-storing soils, such as the Höllenberg's slate [RWV];
  - warm valleys, such as the greenhouse-like Ahr [WoG-Ahr].
- **Heat shortens ripening.** It ripens quickly in warm climates, and warmer regions give more alcohol and less distinctive aromas [OIV pp. 14–15]. In hot German years the risk is too little acidity and too much alcohol, not unripeness. **(H)**
- **Geisenheim's site advice** [HGU]:
  - well-aired sites with soils that warm easily;
  - deep soils with enough water, because the variety reacts to drought.

### 5.2 Flowering and yields

- Cool, humid flowering weather causes *coulure* and *millerandage* [OIV].
- Its natural yields are low: 3–9 t/ha in Burgundy [OIV]. The VDP's private caps are 75, 60 and 50 hl/ha (§3.4).

### 5.3 Clones: why German Pinot changed

The variety mutates readily, which is why so many clone types exist [HGU]. The Federal Plant Variety Office's descriptive list already recorded **52** Spätburgunder clones in 2000 [LVWO-Klone].

**Geisenheim's four clone types** [HGU]:

| Type | Registered examples | Vine and bunch | Wine |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Traditional, compact** | 18 Gm, 20 Gm | Dense bunches. Early botrytis is their weak point, so they need strict spraying and selection. | The classic German type: typical ruby colour; plum and cherry; fine and fragrant when the fruit is healthy |
| **Upright-growing** | 2-2 Gm, 2-6 Gm, 2-9 Gm | Almost no side shoots and an airy canopy, so less leaf work and no leaf removal. Less botrytis, lower malic acid. Labour-saving on steep slopes. | Somewhat darker; blackberry and almond; fine tannin |
| **Loose-bunch** | 1-1 Gm to 1-86 Gm | Long stalks, so bunches dry fast after rain or dew. Little rot, so the grapes can hang longer. | Colour like the traditional clones, a little deeper thanks to the later harvest. Acidity clearly lower than the compact clones'. Plum and cherry. |
| **Small-berried** | 20-13 Gm, 20-16 Gm, 20-19 Gm, 20-20 Gm (selected from 20 Gm) | Many small berries: lower yields and looser bunches, with better botrytis resistance | The deepest colour. Dense, full-bodied and fragrant: blackcurrant, blackberry and sweet cherry, with pepper and clove |

**Other lines of selection:**

- **The Ahr's *Kastenholz* type** has compact, very small, unshouldered bunches. Old vines of it were found in Mayschoß and in the Bad Neuenahrer Sonnenberg. From 2001 Geisenheim selected virus-free vines and planted them in 2003–2004 (clones 401–405 Gm), to re-establish the type as a local variant [HGU].
- **Weinsberg's Mariafeld types** (We M …) go back to a Swiss selection of the 1960s. We M 1 was registered in 1985 and We M 171 in 1999 [LVWO-Klone].
  - In Weinsberg's trials the loose-bunch clones stayed almost free of botrytis. They could be picked **7–14 days later** than the traditional compact clones.
  - The trials compared them with the French compact clones INRA 113, 114 and 115, ENTAV 292 and *Pinot droit* [LVWO-Klone].
- **Freiburg's mixed-berry clones** (e.g. FR 1801) and Geisenheim's small-berried Gm 20-13 were compared with standard clones such as FR 52-86 [WBI-Klone]:
  - lower yields and clearly higher must weights;
  - a much lower rot risk;
  - more skin, better colour and more phenolics;
  - better scores in blind comparisons of the 2001–2005 vintages.

**The quality debate.** **(H)** Clones decide much of a German Spätburgunder's style:

- small-berried and mixed-berry selections, and French clones, for concentration and colour;
- loose-bunch clones for health;
- the old compact clones need perfect, rot-free fruit.

### 5.4 Pests and diseases

- **Downy mildew** (*Peronospora*) was the problem of the wet 2021 and 2024 seasons [DWI-2021; DWI-2024].
- **Spotted-wing drosophila** (*Drosophila suzukii*) [JKI]:
  - first found in Germany in 2011, and nationwide since 2014;
  - it lays eggs mainly in dark-berried varieties, and berry damage (e.g. late powdery mildew, micro-cracks) opens the way;
  - in monitoring, Spätburgunder, Schwarzriesling and Domina were less attractive to it than other susceptible red varieties.
- **Frost.** Spring frost strikes after an early budburst, as in April 2024 (§1.4).

## 6. Winemaking and styles

### 6.1 Two red styles

The DWI contrasts two styles [DWI-SB]:

- **traditional:** mild, low in tannin, brick-red;
- **modern:** a deeper red and more tannin, often barrel-aged.

The DWI's aroma words are cherries and red and dark berries, a peppery spice and almond, with vanilla and cinnamon from barrels.

### 6.2 Red-wine methods

- **Skin fermentation** (*Maischegärung*): the classic method. Choices include cold soaking, whole bunches or destemmed berries, punch-downs or pump-overs, and the length of maceration. *To verify:* sources come with C5's winemaking principles.
- **Thermovinification** (*Maischeerhitzung*) [LVWO-Thermo]:
  - The mash is heated, either briefly to about 80–87 °C or for longer at about 50 °C, then pressed. The juice ferments off the skins.
  - Colour is extracted quickly and oxidising enzymes are inactivated.
  - It suits oxidation-prone varieties such as Spätburgunder and Trollinger.
  - **(H)** It gives soft, fruity, low-tannin reds, sometimes with a cooked-fruit note. Look for it in inexpensive German reds.
- **Oak.** Barrique (§3.7 for the label rules) or larger casks. **(H)** New barrique is a marker of the "modern" style and of top cuvées (§8.3).

### 6.3 Rosé and white wines from red grapes

- **Weißherbst:** one red variety, at least 95 % light-pressed must [WeinV §32(5)].
- **Rosé:** red grapes only.
- **Blanc de Noirs:** red grapes pressed like white wine, with a white wine's colour.
- **Rotling family:** Rotling, Schillerwein (Württemberg), Badisch Rotgold (Baden: Grauburgunder and Spätburgunder), Schieler (Sachsen).
- **In 2025**, Ahr Blanc de Noir from Spätburgunder showed promise [DWI-2025].

### 6.4 Sparkling

Traditional-method Sekt, as Winzersekt (§3.8), Blanc de Noirs and rosé.

### 6.5 Service

The DWI serves Spätburgunder at **16–18 °C** [DWI-SB].

## 8. Tasting profiles

All of §8 is **(H)** unless it cites a source. P3 turns these profiles into `style_trait` relations in the app's own lexicon (T1). Each needs a published source and an expert's check.

### 8.1 The variety

| Aspect | Typical German Spätburgunder |
|---|---|
| Colour | Pale to medium ruby, translucent. A garnet rim appears relatively early. |
| Fruit | Red cherry, sour cherry, strawberry, raspberry and redcurrant. Darker cherry in warm sites and years. |
| Other aromas | Earth and forest floor, dried leaves, sometimes smoke. Almond [DWI-SB]. Whole bunches add herbs, flowers and a stemmy spice. |
| Oak | Vanilla and cinnamon [DWI-SB]; clove, toast and smoke with new barrique. Some modern wines have a struck-match note. |
| Structure | Dry; fresh to high acidity; low to moderate, fine-grained tannin; light to medium body; alcohol usually 12.5–14 %. |

## 10. Blind-tasting playbook

All heuristics **(H)**. They are there for practice. Calibrate them on real wines (§10.4) and trust your own notes over the table.

### 10.1 Decision tree

1. **Is it Pinot?**
   - **Look:** pale to medium, translucent ruby, often an early garnet rim.
   - **Smell:** red fruit (cherry, strawberry, raspberry) with earth, and possibly spice or smoke from oak.
   - **Taste:** fresh acidity; low to moderate, fine tannin; light to medium body.
   - All three agree → Pinot family. Check the look-alikes (§10.2) before committing.
2. **Old World or New World?**
   - Savoury, earthy, restrained fruit, alcohol about 12.5–13.5 % → Old World.
   - Sweet-ripe fruit, cola, vanilla or mocha, alcohol about 14 %+, softer acidity → New World.
3. **If Old World: Germany, Burgundy or a neighbour?**
   - **Germany:** riper fruit or higher alcohol than a village Burgundy in warm years; clear new oak in top cuvées; sometimes smoky reduction; fewer animal notes.
   - **Burgundy:** more earth and savour, higher acidity, firmer fine tannin.
   - **Alsace, Switzerland, Austria, Alto Adige:** lighter, fresher, simpler at the basic level.
4. **If German: which region?** The deciding cues:
   - ripeness and body: Baden, especially the Kaiserstuhl, is fullest;
   - spice and smoky minerality: the Ahr, and Assmannshausen in the Rheingau;
   - finesse and red fruit: Franken and the southern Pfalz.
   
   Producer style can override region, so state the region with a reason (§8.2).
5. **Quality level.** Concentration, length, oak quality and tannin finesse decide it (§8.3).
6. **Age and vintage.** Colour and tertiary notes decide the age (§8.4). Freshness against ripeness decides warm or cool vintage (§7).
7. **Conclusion.** Commit to one answer: grape, country, region, quality level and vintage range. Give the two or three markers that decided it.

### 10.2 Look-alikes

| Wine | What separates it from Spätburgunder **(H)** |
|---|---|
| **Frühburgunder** | The same family, ripening earlier: often deeper colour, softer acidity, riper and darker fruit |
| **Schwarzriesling** (Pinot Meunier), as a red | Lighter; fruity and earthy; less structure and length |
| **St. Laurent** | Darker colour; black cherry; more tannin; Pinot-like texture |
| **Lemberger / Blaufränkisch** | Deeper colour; blackberry and pepper; higher tannin and acidity |
| **Zweigelt** | Sour cherry and pepper; medium body; purple tint |
| **Trollinger** (Schiava / Vernatsch) | Very pale; candied red fruit and almond; very low tannin; light |
| **Dornfelder** | Deep, dark colour; soft; dark berries; low tannin grip; sometimes off-dry |
| **Portugieser** | Pale to medium; light, simple, low acidity |
| **Regent** | Deep colour; dark fruit; a fungus-resistant crossing (*to verify* its breeding) |
| **Gamay** (Beaujolais) | Purple; banana or bubble-gum from carbonic maceration of whole bunches; very low tannin |
| **Nebbiolo** | Pale like Pinot, but high tannin and acidity; tar and roses; an early orange rim |
| **Grenache** | Pale to medium; high alcohol, lower acidity; sweet red fruit and dried herbs |
| **Cabernet Franc** (Loire) | Leafy, green pepper, graphite; firmer tannin |
| **Sangiovese** | Sour cherry and tea; firm, drying tannin; high acidity |

### 10.3 Common traps

- **Calling Burgundy for a warm-vintage German Spätburgunder** (or the reverse). Weigh alcohol, oak style and fruit ripeness together, never one alone.
- **Calling New World for a Kaiserstuhl wine from a hot year.** Look for German acidity and savoury tannin under the ripe fruit.
- **Treating deep colour as "not Pinot".** Frühburgunder, cold soaks, thermovinified wines and small-berried clones can all be darker (§5.3).
- **Misreading a young wine's brown tint as age.** Rotten fruit loses colour and turns red wine brownish [HGU]. Check the nose for mould or earthiness before adding years.
- **Missing the look-alike.** Before concluding Pinot, ask "could this be St. Laurent, Zweigelt or Trollinger?". German flights often hide one.
- **Reading oak as quality.** Heavy new oak on thin fruit suggests a mid-level wine trying hard. Length and tannin quality decide the level.

### 10.4 Calibration flights

1. **Structure ladder:** Trollinger → Spätburgunder Gutswein → Spätburgunder GG → Lemberger → Dornfelder.
2. **Pinot world tour:** Baden or Pfalz · Côte de Beaune village · Central Otago · Willamette Valley · Sonoma Coast.
3. **German regions:** Ahr · Kaiserstuhl · southern Pfalz · Churfranken · Assmannshausen.
4. **Age:** the same wine or producer at about 3 and about 10 years.
5. **Pink and white from red:** Weißherbst · Rosé · Blanc de Noirs · Rotling (Schillerwein or Badisch Rotgold). Judge colour first, then taste.
