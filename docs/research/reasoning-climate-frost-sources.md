## Premises, conclusions and contradictions

- **Wind:** a measured reachable warm inversion, suitable fan siting and operation, calm radiation-frost conditions and matched other heat losses → warmer air mixed to vine level → higher canopy-air temperature from that existing heat. New heat generation by mixing, cooling caused by adding the measured warmer air, and a gain independent of inversion strength contradict separate parts of this route. The scenario excludes heaters, overhead irrigation, strong advective wind and ice fog. It does not establish a fixed temperature gain, uniform coverage, bud survival or complete protection.
- **Sprinkler:** freshwater freezes on uniformly wetted tissue under uninterrupted adequate supply and re-wetting → latent heat released → coated tissue remains near freezing while the specified heat balance persists. Melting-only heat supply, unchanged protection after water stops, and safe shutoff from dry-bulb warming alone contradict phase-change direction, continuous supply and wet-bulb risk respectively. Rate, coverage, wind and minimum wet-bulb conditions must stay within the system's stated design capacity. This does not prescribe an application rate or promise protection if those controls fail.

The starting premises are editorial hypotheses, not claims that the references measured these exact vineyards. Their controlled assumptions make the causal route assessable; they are not operational instructions for an unmeasured frost event. Near-freezing refers to adequately wetted, ice-contact tissue under the supplied balance, not every part of a vine or an unconditional safety threshold.

Two source qualifications are deliberately preserved. The NC State living page's sentence suggesting all active frost methods depend on inversion is overbroad for adequate overhead freezing and is excluded. Its account of warm air being radiated upward is also not used as atmospheric physics. FAO's separate discussion of boundary-layer removal allows a fan contribution outside a strong inversion; these exercises therefore isolate measured warm-air mixing and do not claim that a fan can never help without an inversion. The two systems are kept separate because combining a fan with overhead sprinkling can increase evaporative losses.
