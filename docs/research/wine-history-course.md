# Essential wine-history course

Unregistered teaching text for release work after 0.22.0. It is not learner-visible until the candidate graph in `assets/curriculum/candidates/wine_history_course.yaml` is included. Every factual sentence below is tied to a source already opened for the 0.22.0 pack or, for Columella, opened on 27 September 2026. Claims marked UNRESOLVED are not taught as facts.

This is an essential course, not all of world wine history. It does not add map pins.

## Outcomes

1. Separate a chemical residue, a production installation, and a later legend.
2. Explain what a Roman writer can and cannot tell a modern reader about naming wines.
3. Distinguish a customs treaty from a vineyard demarcation.
4. Keep three different uses of the name Napoleon apart.
5. Explain why a marketing story about sparkling wine is not a production record.
6. State what phylloxera does to vinifera roots and what the cited control is, without inventing the European arrival date.
7. Connect appellation rules, the modern EU definition of wine, Prohibition, and one New World tasting.

## 1. Evidence is not a single "oldest"

McGovern and colleagues report the earliest biomolecular evidence they obtained for grape wine in the Near East, about 6000–5800 BC, from pottery at Shulaveris Gora and Gadachrili Gora in Georgia. That is a residue in pottery. It is not a press, a vat, and jars found together, and the paper does not prove that no older fermented drink will be found.

Areni-1 in Armenia has installations and jars from around 4000 calibrated BCE, with malvidin on pottery from that production context. It is later than the Georgian residues. Calling it "the oldest wine" collapses two different kinds of evidence.

Why it matters now: a label, a museum caption, or a guest who says "the first wine" is usually compressing a method. The useful reply is to ask whether the claim is a residue, an installation, or a story.

Misconception: the most complete early cellar is the earliest wine. Comparison: Georgia answers "how early is the chemistry?"; Areni answers "how early is a working installation?"

## 2. Rome names slopes; it does not write an appellation

In Natural History book 14, Pliny says no wine of his own day ranked higher than Falernian. He describes a dry, a sweet, and a light style, and a slope order of Caucinian on the hilltops, Faustian halfway up, and Falernian at the bottom. That is a literary ranking in one author's present. It is not a decree fixing grapes, yields, or a minimum strength.

Columella, writing on vines in De Re Rustica book 3, treats the vine as neither a tree nor a shrub and says it responds to care in nearly every country except icy cold and burning heat. He tells the farmer to match the variety to the place: a vine for level ground that can take mist and frost, a vine for a hillside that can take drought and wind; a slender vine on fat land, a prolific vine on lean land. He says climate — cold or warm, dry or wet, windy or calm — is a more powerful consideration than the soil choice alone. He also says cuttings brought from another district are less at home than stock raised on the farm, and that a vine's quality is not proved by one vintage.

Why it matters now: matching variety to site, and distrusting a single lucky year, are still how growers think. The legal apparatus that later fixes area, grapes, and yield is not in these Roman books.

Misconception: Falernian was an appellation contrôlée. Comparison: Pliny ranks a famous wine of his day; Columella gives husbandry. Neither is the 1935 French rule or the EU definition of wine.

## 3. A tax preference is not a boundary

The commercial treaty concluded in Lisbon on 27 December 1703 admitted British woollen cloth into Portugal and admitted wine of Portuguese growth into Britain at a duty one third below the duty on the same measure of French wine, in peace or war. It does not draw a vineyard line and it does not define Port.

A royal charter of 10 September 1756 instituted the Companhia Geral da Agricultura das Vinhas do Alto Douro to control quality and prices and to demarcate vineyards. The Portuguese monuments inventory notes earlier delimited zones, including Chianti in 1716 and Tokaj in 1737. The charter is a regulated company demarcation. It is not the first line ever drawn around vines, and it is not today's boundary by itself.

Why it matters now: "the English market" and "the first demarcation" get fused into one story. The duty explains a commercial advantage. The charter explains an attempt to police what was shipped. They are fifty years and one problem apart.

Misconception: the Methuen treaty created the Douro. Comparison: 1703 changes the tax; 1756 creates an institution with a demarcation.

UNRESOLVED: Eleanor of Aquitaine and any volume of Gascon trade. Not taught until a page is open.

## 4. Three Napoleons

Napoleon I's Berlin Decree of 21 November 1806 declared the British Isles blockaded and prohibited commerce and correspondence with them. It is a trade blockade. It classifies no château.

For the Paris Universal Exhibition of 1855, Napoleon III asked Bordeaux's brokers to rank wines by reputation and by the prices of recent vintages. The ranking covers Médoc red wines, the Graves red wine Château Haut-Brion, and the sweet wines of Sauternes and Barsac. It does not include Saint-Émilion or Pomerol.

On the BNIC list, the word Napoleon on a Cognac is an ageing mention of at least six years, and the age is the youngest eau-de-vie. It is not evidence that either emperor made the bottle.

Why it matters now: one proper name is doing three jobs. A guest, a flashcard, or a label can switch jobs without warning.

Misconception: Napoleon classified the Médoc, or a Cognac marked Napoleon is an imperial bottling. Comparison: 1806 is war and trade; 1855 is a price-and-reputation list; the Cognac word is a minimum age.

## 5. Sparkling wine has a paper trail and a later story

On 17 December 1662 Christopher Merrett told the Royal Society that English wine-coopers used large amounts of sugar and molasses to make wines brisk and sparkling. That is a documented description of deliberately making wine sparkling. It is not a distillation method.

Pierre Pérignon became cellar master at Hautvillers after that paper. The Royal College of Physicians account says his task included preventing bubbles. That account does not make him the inventor of the method Merrett described, and the later slogan that he was tasting the stars is not part of the 1662 paper.

Why it matters now: a house name can honour a cellar master without that man having invented the style. The production question is who described the addition of sugar, and when.

Misconception: one monk invented Champagne. Comparison: a dated paper versus a later attribution. UNRESOLVED: every later French archival claim. This lesson does not say no French maker ever worked on bubbles. It says the inventor story is not supported by the 1662 record or by the college account of his task.

## 6. Phylloxera, as far as the opened page goes

Grape phylloxera is an aphid-like insect that feeds on the roots of Vitis vinifera and can stunt or kill the vine. University of California guidance treats resistant American rootstocks, including Vitis riparia, Vitis rupestris, and their hybrids, as the effective control, and it does not treat insecticides as a complete remedy.

Why it matters now: own-rooted vinifera and grafted vinifera are different planting decisions where this insect is present. The page supports the biology and the control. It does not date the European epidemic.

UNRESOLVED: the year phylloxera was identified in France. Do not teach 1863 from memory.

## 7. Law, a ban, and one tasting

The French wine code, in the text published for 25 November 1967, records a category of controlled appellations of origin for wines and eaux-de-vie and ties that category to the decree of 30 July 1935. The stated conditions include the production area, the grape varieties, the yield per hectare, a minimum alcoholic strength from vinification without enrichment, and the methods of growing and making the wine. That is a control of how a name may be used. It is not a guarantee that every named bottle tastes the same.

In the consolidated text of Regulation (EU) No 1308/2013, wine means the product obtained exclusively from the total or partial alcoholic fermentation of fresh grapes, whether or not crushed, or of grape must. Ordinary minimum actual strength is 8.5 percent in zones A and B and 9 percent elsewhere, with a derogation to 4.5 percent for some protected wines, and total strength is normally not above 15 percent, with stated exceptions. Wine in that definition is not a distilled spirit.

The US National Archives records the Eighteenth Amendment as passed by Congress on 18 December 1917 and ratified on 16 January 1919, with national Prohibition in effect from 16 January 1920. The Twenty-first Amendment was ratified on 5 December 1933. Its first section repeals the Eighteenth. Its second section still prohibits transporting intoxicating liquors into a state in violation of that state's law. A national ban and a state power are not the same rule.

On 24 May 1976 Steven Spurrier and Patricia Gallagher organized a blind tasting in Paris. Nine French judges compared six California Chardonnays with four white Burgundies, and six California Cabernet Sauvignons with four red Bordeaux. The Smithsonian records that the 1973 Chateau Montelena Chardonnay and the 1973 Stag's Leap Wine Cellars S.L.V. Cabernet Sauvignon scored first in their groups. That is one tasting. It is not a permanent ranking of every wine, and it does not by itself explain Prohibition or repeal.

Why it matters now: "the law", "the ban", and "California won" are three different scales. Appellation law limits a name. Prohibition removed a legal trade and its repeal returned that trade to a federal-and-state split. The Paris tasting is a result on one day.

Misconception: repeal made American wine better, or the 1976 tasting rewrote French appellation law. Comparison: a statute, a constitutional ban and repeal, and a blind tasting.

UNRESOLVED: the Italian DOC law of 12 July 1963. Not taught until the statute page is open.

## Coverage review

The seven outcomes above are taught with a comparison, a misconception, and, for four of them, an applied case. What is still too shallow or absent, even if every row were valid:

- Medieval institutions and any quantified Gascon trade. Eleanor is unresolved.
- Bottle glass, cork, and the shift from amphora to bottle. Not opened, so not taught.
- The year and path of phylloxera in Europe. The biology is taught. The date is not.
- Clos de Vougeot and other monastic vineyards. Unresolved.
- Italian DOC in 1963. Unresolved.
- A New World legal institution other than Prohibition and one tasting. Unresolved.
- South America, South Africa, and Australia as producing countries. Not this course.

Those gaps do not make the taught distinctions false. They mean the course is not finished history.
